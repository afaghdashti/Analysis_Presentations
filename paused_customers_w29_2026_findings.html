Paused customers

Week 29, 2026 (vs. Week 29, 2025) · Afagh Dashti

The active base fell 9.6% year on year and the share of it that is paused did not move at all (63.1% → 63.0%). But the composition of that paused pool changed materially. Short pauses fell 17.3% while long pauses fell only 1.9%, and customers dormant for 27 weeks or more grew 22.5% in absolute terms against a shrinking base. Among long pausers who gave a reason, price is cited at 1.9× the short-pauser rate, and the price share rises monotonically the deeper the dormancy. Recipe is cited by 1.4%. Split by loyalty, long-pause risk peaks in the middle of the curve rather than at the start of it, and every band from 2 to 50 lifetime boxes deteriorated by more than 5 percentage points — a move the reported increase understates, because the base simultaneously shifted toward the lowest-risk band.

01What the paused base is made of

Of 205,408 active, eligible HF Canada customers in 2026-W29, 129,435 were paused. Splitting that paused group by how many consecutive weeks each customer had actually been paused up to and including W29 gives two roughly equal halves, with the long side now the larger of the two.

02The pause rate is flat. That is the misleading number.

Read at the top line, nothing happened: 63.1% of the base was paused in 2025-W29 and 63.0% in 2026-W29. Every absolute count fell, because the base fell 9.6%. The movement is entirely in the mix, and the mix moved in one direction — away from short, recoverable pauses and toward entrenched dormancy.

Figure 2 — Year-on-year change by segment. The reference line is the change in the active base (−9.6%). Segments to the right of it grew relative to the base; segments to the left shrank faster than it.

Three things stand out against the −9.6% base decline. New pause inflow fell faster than the base (−16.1%), which is good. Short pausers fell faster still (−17.3%). But long pausers barely moved (−1.9%), so they now account for 53.6% of everyone paused, up from 49.3% — a 4.3 percentage point shift. Long pausers as a share of the whole active base rose from 31.1% to 33.8%.

03Growth is concentrated in deep dormancy

Breaking the long pausers down by exactly how many consecutive weeks they had been paused shows the shift is not spread evenly. Every bucket under 27 weeks shrank. Both buckets above 27 weeks grew, in absolute terms, while the base they came from was falling.

04Why they paused — long vs. short

Pause reason is captured once, in an optional survey at the moment a customer presses pause. A customer 30 weeks into a pause answered that survey 30 weeks ago, so the reason had to be recovered from the start of each customer's own pause spell rather than read off the W29 rows. Coverage is therefore partial and must be stated.

Coverage, before the percentages. 47,588 of the 69,399 long pausers (68.6%) have a submitted reason on file; 21,811 (31.4%) do not. For short pausers coverage is 51.0% (30,648 of 60,036). All reason percentages below are shares of respondents, not of the full paused base. The long-vs-short coverage gap is mechanical — long pausers have had a longer window in which to have answered the survey once — and should not be read as an engagement difference. Coverage is, however, stable across the four dormancy buckets (67.2%, 69.0%, 70.4%, 68.8%), which is what makes the depth gradient in Section 05 safe to interpret.

Short pauses are logistics; long pauses are economics. Away is the second-largest reason for short pausers at 28.9% and roughly half that for long pausers at 14.1% — people who go away come back. The two price reasons combined run 19.6% among long pausers against 10.5% among short, a 1.87× gap. Frequency is the single largest reason in both groups at around 30% and does not discriminate between them, which points at plan cadence rather than anything specific to a given week. Recipe is cited by 1.4% of long pausers and 1.5% of short pausers.

06Long-pause risk peaks in the middle of the loyalty curve

Splitting long pausers by lifetime boxes (clv_loyalty) shows risk is not monotonic in loyalty. It rises steeply from the first box, peaks in the middle of the curve, then falls away. The 51+ band carries the lowest long-pause rate of any band, and also the lowest overall pause rate (51.1% vs. 63.0% for the base). The shape is the same in both years, so the ordering is stable rather than a single-week artefact.

The reported increase understates the deterioration

Every band from [2–5] through [21–50] worsened by 5.1 to 5.6 percentage points, yet the total long-pause rate rose only 3.3pp. That gap is a composition effect. The [+51] band grew from 23.9% to 28.2% of the active base, and because it carries the lowest long-pause rate, its increased weight pulls the average down. Holding the 2025 band mix fixed and applying 2026 within-band rates gives 36.3%, not 35.2% — so the underlying deterioration is roughly 4.4pp, of which about 1.1pp is hidden by the mix shift. The base is getting healthier on paper while behaviour within every band is getting worse.

All of the growth is in the top two bands

The long-pauser pool shrank by 1,360 overall. That net figure hides two opposite movements: bands [1] to [11–20] lost long pausers roughly in line with their shrinking bases, while [21–50] and [+51] added them. [+51] is the only band whose active base grew at all, and its long-pauser count grew four times faster than its base.

How to read this section.

Selection, not protection. clv_loyalty is cumulative lifetime boxes, so it is strongly correlated with tenure. Reaching 51+ boxes requires having already not long-paused-and-churned. The low long-pause rate in the top band is therefore partly survivorship, and the shape of the curve must not be read as evidence that loyalty protects against long pausing. Separating the two would need a tenure-controlled cut, which is not done here.

Footnote on the exclusion. Customers with no box yet (the [0] band) are excluded from this section. They are pre-first-delivery accounts whose recording changed year on year — their base grew 38.7% and their paused count more than tripled, entirely in short pauses — so including them would distort the loyalty comparison. They are 0.2% of long pausers, and every conclusion in this section holds with or without them.

07What the data supports, and what it does not

Supported by the data in this document

  • The pause rate is unchanged year on year. Any narrative built on "more people are pausing" is not supported — 63.1% → 63.0%.
  • The paused pool is ageing. Every dormancy bucket below 27 weeks shrank; both above it grew, and 25,253 customers have now been continuously paused for half a year or more.
  • Long pausers are the only paused segment holding its level against a 9.6% base decline (−1.9%), so they are a growing share of both the paused pool (53.6%) and the total active base (33.8%).
  • Among respondents, price discriminates strongly between long and short pauses (1.87×) and strengthens with dormancy depth. Recipe does not discriminate and is small in both groups (~1.4%).
  • Away is a short-pause reason. Its share halves between short and long pausers and declines monotonically with spell length.
  • Long-pause risk is not monotonic in loyalty. It peaks in the middle of the curve (48.0% at 6–10 boxes) and is lowest in the 51+ band (19.5%). The same ordering holds in both years.
  • Every band from [2–5] to [21–50] worsened by more than 5pp, but the total rose only 3.3pp because the base shifted toward the lowest-risk band. The headline understates the underlying move by roughly 1pp.
  • All growth in long pausers sits in [21–50] (+489) and [+51] (+1,916), against a 3,765 loss across [1] to [11–20]. [+51] is the only band whose base grew (+5.0%).

Not established by this analysis

  • Causality. These are stated reasons at pause time, from an optional survey. They are not a measured driver of pause duration.
  • The 31.4% of long pausers with no reason on file. They are not missing at random — non-response is a choice — so the price share should be treated as a floor, not a point estimate.
  • Reason recency. A 53+ week pauser's answer describes the price and menu environment of a year ago, not today's. Recency-weight before pricing anything off it.
  • Year-on-year reason mix. Not reported here. The survey table starts at 2024-W01, so spell-start reasons for the deepest 2025 pausers fall outside coverage, and the resulting comparison would be confounded by differential coverage rather than real change.
  • Whether the deep-dormancy pool is recoverable. Not tested. No reactivation or win-back response data is in scope in this document.
  • That loyalty protects against long pausing. The curve in Section 06 is confounded with tenure by construction — reaching a high lifetime-box band requires having already survived the early-life long-pause risk. The low rate in [+51] is partly survivorship and cannot be separated from a genuine loyalty effect without a tenure-controlled cut.
  • Why the [2–5] and [6–10] bases fell hardest (−22.0% and −19.3%). This is consistent with lower acquisition, faster early churn, or customers ageing out of the band, and no acquisition-cohort data is in scope here to distinguish them.
  • The precise peak of the loyalty risk curve. The bands are uneven in width; the peak is located somewhere in [2–20] and [6–10] is only the bucket that happens to contain it.
Definitions used throughout.

Active base — customers with customer_status in the active/eligible set for bob_entity_code = 'CA', brand_code = 'HF'. Excludes Former and Ineligible.

Paused — customer_sub_status in (Active User Paused, Active Interval Paused). Interval-paused is a small minority (~2.4k/week).

Long pauser — realised 6 or more consecutive paused weeks up to and including the observation week, built by gaps-and-islands on weekly status. This is deliberately not the pause_intervals field, which records the pause length the customer selected. That field averages 12.5–18.2 weeks, against a Little's-Law implied realised average of roughly 6.0 weeks (131,641 stock ÷ 22,033 weekly inflow), so it overstates duration.

Newly paused — consecutive paused run of exactly 1 in the observation week. Cross-checked against previous_customer_sub_status, matched exactly.

Short pauser — realised run of 1 to 5 weeks. Includes the newly paused by construction.

Loyalty band — clv_loyalty, cumulative mealboxes since first activation, taken at the observation week. Chosen over ccv_loyalty (boxes since last reactivation, which resets and so blurs tenure with recency) and over any trailing-52-week measure, which is mechanically confounded with pausing: a long pauser necessarily has a low 52-week box count, making that cut near-tautological for this analysis.

Sources and validation

Volumes and dormancy depth: glue.marketing_analytics_main.aca_customer_base_pause_unpause_customer_cluster_granular, deduplicated from customer × plan to customer × week. Reconciles to glue.global_bi_business.customer_status_weekly exactly on distinct customers and within 5–8 customers on paused counts. Note this table recodes customer_status to Paused/Running/Active, so filtering it to 'Active' silently drops every paused customer.

Pause reason: glue.marketing_analytics_main.aca_weekly_pause_reasons_surveylevels_2024_onwards, filtered to country = 'CA' and event_action LIKE '%surveySubmit%' (confirmed submissions, not pre-submit clicks). Keyed on menu_week — the delivery week being paused — which is what makes spell-start recovery possible. The granular table above carries both customer_nk and customer_id, so no bridge table is required.

Not used, and why: aca_pause_reason_segmentation_agg_metrics has no customer key at all and cannot be attributed to a named cohort; it also records pause actions (~28–30k/week) rather than the paused base (~129k/week), so it misses roughly 80% of paused customers. pause_segmentation_master_data has customer_uuid, pre-built reason ratios and a recent_pause_streak column and would be the better table, but its coverage stops at 2026-W12.

Validation performed: (1) reason join returns exactly 69,399 long and 60,036 short, matching the independently derived W29 counts — the join adds and drops nobody; (2) all four dormancy buckets reconcile to their standalone counts once the 0.5% multi-select overlap is accounted for; (3) reason vocabulary matches the aca_pause_reason tables exactly, 7 values; (4) survey coverage checked per bucket and found flat at 67–70%; (5) new-pause flow cross-checked against previous_customer_sub_status and matched in all weeks tested.

Loyalty bands (Section 06): clv_loyalty read from the same granular table at the observation week, so no orders-fact join is involved. With the [0] band included, bands sum exactly to the verified totals in both years (227,275 / 70,724 and 205,408 / 69,399) with no null band; Section 06 then excludes [0], leaving 221,050 / 70,624 and 196,774 / 69,264. The mix statement is a direct standardisation — 2025 band weights applied to 2026 within-band rates — giving +4.38pp within-band and −1.13pp mix, net +3.25pp. It is +4.26pp / −1.59pp on the full base including [0], so the conclusion does not depend on the exclusion.
