When a long pause ends, almost half of the first boxes are ones the customer never touched

Long-pauser deep dive, question 1. Pauses of 6+ weeks that ended between 2025-W41 and 2026-W40. Afagh Dashti, October 2026

Over the last 12 months, 256,139 pauses of six weeks or more came to an end in HF Canada, across 171,885 customers. Three in four ended with a box. Among customers still on the active base that week, 44.8% of those first boxes were surprise boxes: the customer did not swap a recipe, add an add-on or customize anything. For a customer who also had a box the week before, the same no-action rate is 19.7%. A first box after a long pause is about 2.3 times as likely to go untouched, and the longer the pause, the higher the share: 40.1% after 6–12 weeks, 60.5% after a year or more. Only 3.0% of surprise boxes followed an unpause (a paused delivery switched back on), against 50.1% of boxes the customer acted on.

01How long pauses end

A pause is in scope if it lasted six or more consecutive weeks and its exit week (the first week the customer was no longer paused) fell between 2025-W41 and 2026-W40. That makes this a flow of pause endings across the whole year, not a snapshot of one week. Pauses still running at 2026-W40 are not included.

73.6% of these pauses ended with a box. 21.0% ended in a cancellation with no box, and 5.4% ended with the customer back on the base but no box shipped (section 06 explains why). The orange segments are the surprise boxes.

They differ because 34.5% of customers had two or more long pauses end within the 12 months, and those repeat cyclers account for 56.1% of all long pauses. The surprise share is almost the same either way, so the result is not driven by a small group of customers cycling in and out.

Why the headline uses customers still on the base. 17,814 of the 188,505 box exits (9.5%) were delivered in a week when the customer cancelled, and 84.9% of those were surprise boxes. Including them lifts the surprise share to 48.6%. They are real shipped boxes (section 06), but they would also distort the next-week view in section 05, so the conservative on-base share of 44.8% is used as the headline throughout.

02Untouched boxes are twice as common after a long pause

Some boxes go untouched in any week. Plenty of continuing customers are happy with the default recipes. The fair comparison is therefore the no-action rate for other boxes in the same 12 months, using exactly the same definition.

All HF Canada boxes = every CA mealbox order in the same 12 months (4,211,728 boxes), whatever the customer did the week before. Short and long pause bars are first boxes for customers still on the base. Source: long_pausers_surprise_box_TEMPLATE.sql, main query and Query 2a, on facts_recipes_ordered_enriched.

A first box after a short pause (1–5 weeks) is untouched at 22.8%, close to an ordinary week. The jump comes with the long pause: 44.8%, or 2.3 times the continuing-customer rate. When a long-pause customer does act on their first box, it is almost always a recipe swap. Of the 188,505 box exits, 95,143 had a swap, 1,567 had only an add-on and 131 had only a customization.

Check on the definition. If a surprise box really reflects no customer action, it should rarely follow an unpause, where a paused delivery is switched back on. The delivery status table agrees. Only 3.0% of long-pause surprise boxes followed an unpause, against 50.1% of boxes where the customer acted (2.8% vs 47.9% per customer). The other half of actioned boxes came from a pause that simply ended, after which the customer still went in and picked recipes. Two limits: this table does not record who switched the delivery back on, and a pause that simply ends may well have been planned by the customer. So the flag supports the surprise-box definition but does not measure intent. Source: delivery_status_weekly.is_unpaused (documented in related tables as "the customer previously paused and then reactivated their delivery"), Query 5.

03The longer the pause, the less likely the customer touches the box

The surprise share rises steadily with pause length, from 40.1% after 6–12 weeks to 60.5% after 53 weeks or more, three times the ordinary-week rate. Counting each customer once gives the same pattern.

Figure 3. Surprise share of first boxes for customers still on the base, by how long the pause lasted. The dashed line is the 19.7% continuing-customer rate. Source: long_pausers_surprise_box_TEMPLATE.sql, main query and Query 3.

04Long pausers leave about twice as many boxes untouched, whatever their number of past boxes

Could the high surprise share just come from newer customers, who touch their boxes less in any week? The data says no. Each long pauser is compared with ordinary customers (box the week before too) who had received the same number of boxes:

  • In every group, the first box after a long pause is left untouched 2.0 to 2.6 times as often as in an ordinary week. For example, 57.2% against 27.9% for customers with one previous box, and 35.6% against 17.7% for customers with more than 50.
  • If long pausers behaved like ordinary customers with the same number of boxes, about 19.8% of their first boxes would be untouched, almost the same as the 19.7% ordinary-week rate. So being newer explains almost none of the gap.

Within long pausers, the untouched share is lower for customers with more boxes (57.2% down to 35.6%). About half of that drop (10.2 points of 21.6) also shows up in ordinary weeks, and the rest may be survivorship (see the note below). So this chart does not show that loyalty makes customers more likely to engage with their first box. Counting each customer once gives the same picture.

Read the loyalty pattern with care. Lifetime boxes go hand in hand with tenure. A customer with more than 50 boxes has already been through many pause cycles and stayed, so the lower rate in that group may be partly survivorship. It is not evidence that loyalty makes customers engage with their first box. Separating the two would need a tenure-controlled cut.

05The week after: most customers pause again, whether or not they touched the box

What customers do next is one clue to whether the restart was planned. After a surprise box, 81.6% are paused again the following week, and only 15.2% take a second box. But long-pause customers who did act on their first box (swapped, added or customized) also mostly re-pause (74.9%). A long pause usually ends in a single box either way. The difference is in who stays: customers who acted are about 1.5 times as likely to take a second box (23.5% vs 15.2%).

06Two edge cases

Boxes delivered in the week the customer cancelled. 17,814 first boxes went to customers who cancelled that same week. 84.9% of them were surprise boxes, the highest rate of any group in this analysis. The finance table confirms they are real: 97.9% shipped and 92.4% shipped and not refunded. The cancellation record confirms all of them cancelled in that week. The most likely story is a customer who was resumed, did not engage, and cancelled after the cutoff, so the box still went out.

Back on the base but no box. 13,666 long pauses ended with the customer running but no box in the orders fact. The finance table shows 83.1% of these were orders that were created and then not shipped, most often because the payment failed (39.5%). Another 15.3% had no order at all. The delivery stayed planned, so these are not late skips. They are kept as their own exit type.

07What this suggests

After a long pause, 44.8% of first boxes go untouched, 2.3 times the 19.7% of an ordinary week. The share rises with the length of the pause, from 40.1% after 6–12 weeks to 60.5% after a year or more, and it is about twice the ordinary rate whatever the customer's number of past boxes. After a surprise box, 81.6% pause again the following week and only 15.2% take a second box, against 23.5% for customers who acted on their first box.

This is descriptive, not causal. A surprise box is a signal that the restart may not have been planned, not proof: some customers will have been happy with the defaults, and some pauses were meant to end when they did. The natural next step is a test rather than more cuts of this data. For example, a reminder a few days before a pause of 13+ weeks ends, with a randomised holdout, measured on second-box rate and on cancellation in the following four weeks. Volume is not a constraint. Pauses of 13 weeks or more end with about 1,300 first boxes on the base a week, so there is enough volume for a test with a holdout. A power calculation should be part of the test design.

The second question of this deep dive, how much of the rise in pausing is customers pausing instead of cancelling, is not covered here.

08Definitions

Long pause. Six or more consecutive weeks as Active User Paused or Active Interval Paused on aca_customer_base_pause_unpause_customer_cluster_granular (CA, HF), using the same streak logic as the W29 paused-customer analysis. Short pause: 1–5 weeks.

Exit week. The first week after the last paused week. In scope when it falls between 2025-W41 and 2026-W40.

Exit outcome. Box: a mealbox exists in facts_recipes_ordered_enriched that week. Cancelled: no box and no row on the granular table, which holds active statuses only. All 53,800 of these, all 17,814 boxes delivered in a cancellation week, and all 2,968 next-week cancellations in section 05 show customer_status = 'Former' with cancellation_recency = 0 in customer_cancellation_recency_weekly, so they are plan cancellations in that week. On the base, no box: everything else.

Surprise box. A box where recipes_swapped_in and recipes_swapped_out are empty on every mealbox row, there are no add-on rows, and there are no item_category = 'Customization' rows. Premium surcharge rows do not count as an action, because without a swap they can be assigned by the system.

Lifetime boxes. clv_loyalty at the last paused week, so the first box itself is not counted.

Baseline. The same no-action rule applied to every HF Canada mealbox order with a delivery week from 2025-W41 to 2026-W40 (4,211,728 boxes), split by whether the customer had a box the week before. The continuing-customer baseline (2,631,023 boxes) is the subset with a box the previous week too.

Per customer. Each customer's first long pause that ended in the window.
